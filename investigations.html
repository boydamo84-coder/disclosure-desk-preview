Roadmap phases A + D · Methodology

How we assess a UAP claim

Our evidence review deliberately separates what was reported, what a surviving record establishes, what might explain it, and what cannot currently be resolved. An official archive establishes that a record exists and documents what it says; it does not independently verify every claim within the record. Likewise, an unexplained report is not automatically evidence for any particular origin.

  1. Provenance: identify the earliest accessible document, its institution, author where known, date, catalogue reference and later versions. Mark second-hand summaries as such.
  2. Timeline: distinguish an event date from the date of its report, release, publication or later retelling. Flag uncertain dates and time zones.
  3. Evidence inventory: list whether raw imagery, sensor metadata, measurements, contemporaneous notes and independently obtained testimony can actually be inspected. Missing data is reported, not silently filled in.
  4. Alternative explanations: ask what ordinary objects, optical effects, environmental conditions or instrument limits could produce a similar account. A possibility is not the same as a demonstrated explanation.
  5. Confidence and limitations: describe confidence in individual documents and observations, not an invented “alien probability”. Uncertainty can persist even when the case is interesting.
  6. Correction path: revisit claims when better primary sources emerge; retain source links and explain material changes in later revisions.

This is an evidence-completeness framework, not a scoring system for how strange an incident appears. Private witness information and unpublished research are not included in these public reviews.

Evidence review 01 · UK · 1980

Rendlesham Forest: what the official record preserves

Research review: 13 November 2026 · Source scope: UK National Archives collection guide and catalogue description

Record exists · explanation not established here

What is actually documented?

The UK National Archives identifies the December 1980 Rendlesham Forest incident in Suffolk as one of the country's best-known reported UFO events. Its collection overview discusses correspondence attributed to Lieutenant Colonel Charles Halt and the reported investigation of lights near RAF Woodbridge. Importantly, that description is a guide to the archive's holdings. It is not a modern technical reconstruction of the night sky, nor does it confirm any claimed craft.

The Archives says it holds a single-sheet report of the incident itself, while much of the additional surviving paperwork concerns later enquiries and correspondence from the public and press. This distinction matters: the number of pages discussing an incident is not the same as the number of independent contemporaneous observations. A large file can reflect decades of interest rather than stronger original evidence.

What the record cannot establish by itself

The public collection description does not provide a complete collection of instrument readings, original multi-sensor recordings or a fully reconstructable sequence of observations. It therefore cannot, on its own, determine the physical source of the lights. Later witness accounts, interviews or reconstructions may be useful leads, but must be dated, attributed and distinguished from material written at the time.

Competing interpretations to test

A responsible reconstruction would test reported sightlines, timings and local environmental conditions against possible astronomical, navigational, aircraft and ground-light sources. None of those possibilities is asserted here as a demonstrated solution. Equally, the label “unexplained” in a report would not establish an extraterrestrial interpretation.

What researchers should check next

  • Open the National Archives record associated with the Halt correspondence and check its original wording and document date.
  • Build a chronology that separates 1980 observations from later statements and official responses.
  • Record what source material is genuinely available for independent examination, and what is known only through later summaries.

Evidence review 02 · US historical records

Project Blue Book: an archive is evidence of an investigation, not a universal verdict

Research review: 13 November 2026 · Source scope: US National Archives research pages

Declassified source collection · individual cases vary

What the archive establishes

Project Blue Book was the US Air Force's investigation programme into UFO reports. The National Archives explains that the programme ended in 1969 and that its declassified records were transferred for public research. These records are valuable because they preserve official investigative activity, historical reporting practices and case documents that can be checked against contemporary claims.

But the existence of a Blue Book file does not mean its original report was independently confirmed, and the closure of the programme does not provide a case-specific explanation for every entry. Archive users need to inspect what a particular folder actually contains: a first-person witness account, a later narrative, an investigator's summary, correspondence, or a conclusion drawn from the evidence available at that time.

Questions worth asking before sharing a case

When an online post cites “Project Blue Book”, it should identify a catalogue record or specific document—not merely invoke the programme's name. Was the event recorded immediately? Did investigators preserve original measurement data or photographs? Can a supposed match or dismissal be checked against the actual document? Is the item being described a single allegation or several independent reports?

We should also separate the archive's access history from the evidence itself. A record becoming public decades later is not, without further facts, evidence of a suppressed extraordinary conclusion. Conversely, missing measurements or weak historical investigations can leave legitimate questions unanswered.

Next research step

Start from a document-level identifier, capture the original wording and date, then annotate factual claims against the source. A high-quality public case page should link directly to the relevant National Archives item where available, show the uncertainty that remains, and correct errors in later reporting rather than amplifying them.

Research review 03 · scientific evidence standards

NASA's 2023 UAP study: better data, not a retrospective solution

Research review: 13 November 2026 · Source scope: NASA's UAP study announcement and report

Research recommendations · not a historical case assessment

What NASA actually commissioned

NASA published the report of its independent UAP study team on 14 September 2023. The team examined how scientific methods and available data sources could improve future study of unusual observations. NASA's report explicitly explains that the exercise was not an investigation of every historical UAP incident. Treating it as a verdict on a named past case would go beyond its scope.

Why this changes how we investigate

The practical lesson is not simply that researchers need more reports. They need observations whose origin, timing, calibration and context can be evaluated. A dramatic image may be insufficient if the original footage, camera geometry, sensor characteristics or environmental conditions are unavailable. Independent measurements can help distinguish a genuinely unknown observation from one where critical context was never collected.

That means historical cases and current sightings may deserve different kinds of conclusions. For a recent video with intact metadata, investigators might test identifiable technical explanations. For an old incident documented primarily through correspondence, the responsible finding may be that the documents establish a historical report but lack enough data for a physical determination.

What we can change at Disclosure Desk

  • Give public investigations an evidence inventory that displays what's available and what's missing, without substituting an arbitrary likelihood percentage.
  • Keep the original link and case chronology beside every meaningful analytical claim.
  • Label suggested related sightings as leads, not verified corroboration, until source independence and timing are checked.

Editorial standards

Our promises to readers

These notes are independent reviews written for Disclosure Desk, not endorsements by the institutions linked above. An archive item, testimony, news story, hypothesis and verified measurement are different kinds of source. We label them accordingly and avoid declaring an unknown event either extraordinary or conclusively mundane without supporting evidence.

We link outward to original sources rather than claiming ownership of their work. Material corrections should explain what changed and identify the revised source; new evidence may change an assessment. We do not publish someone's private Boards, documents, chat, precise witness location or personal details as a research article without their explicit authorisation.

Found a factual error, broken citation or missing primary record? Send an editorial correction with the article title, claim and a verifiable source. This page contains initial research briefs, not completed determinations for every case mentioned.
